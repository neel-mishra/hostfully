# How To Build a Fanbase: From Followers to True Fans

Most creators have an audience problem, but not the one they think. They have followers. What they need is fans.

Kevin Kelly's "1,000 True Fans" is 18 years old and still the best business plan in the creator economy. The thesis: you don't need millions of followers. You need a relatively small number of people who will buy anything you make. Subscribers get your content. Customers buy a product. Fans do both — and recruit more fans without being asked.

The distinction matters because the economics are wildly different. Matt Brown runs Extra Points, a newsletter about the business side of college sports. He has 27,000 total readers. Only 2,000 pay. That 2,000 generates over $200K annually. Depth of engagement beats breadth of list every time.

## Followers vs. Fans: The Gap Nobody Talks About

A follower is someone who clicked a button once. A fan is someone who forwards your newsletter to three people, defends you in a comment section, and buys your next product sight unseen.

EssentiallySports, one of the top 10 sports publishers in the U.S., had massive reach across social platforms. Millions of followers. But they were renting their audience from algorithms. Co-founder Suryansh Tibarewal put it bluntly: "We were operating in an environment where platforms controlled distribution."

Their fix: a network of hyper-niche sports newsletters — NASCAR, tennis, golf, NFL — that now reach 1.5 million subscribers with 45%+ open rates. Zero paid acquisition spend. The audience shifted from passive social followers to active email subscribers who open, click, and reply.

That shift is what separates a following from a fanbase.

## The Mistakes That Kill Fanbase Growth

### Chasing vanity metrics

20,000 subscribers who never open your emails are worth less than 2,000 who read every word. Open rates, reply rates, and referral rates tell you more about fan loyalty than raw subscriber count.

### Platform dependency

If your entire audience lives on Instagram or TikTok, you don't have a fanbase. You have a lease that can be terminated at any time by an algorithm update. Dave Jorgenson, the former Washington Post TikTok star, understood this. He launched LNI Media (a local news newsletter) specifically to convert social followers into email subscribers he could actually reach. The newsletter hit five-figure readership in months with a 60% open rate.

### Ignoring early supporters

Your first 100 subscribers are your most valuable. They showed up before you had proof. Neglect them while chasing new subscribers and you lose the advocates who would have done your marketing for you.

### Being everywhere, owning nowhere

Posting across six platforms means you're mediocre on all of them. Go deep on one or two channels where your audience actually spends time.

## Four Strategies That Build Real Fans

### 1. Email as the ownership layer

Email is the only channel where you control the relationship. No algorithm decides who sees your message. No platform can revoke access to your subscribers.

Chloe Lin runs Arigato Investor, a finance newsletter. She sends nearly daily and made $250K in a single year — not from ads alone, but from high-ticket coaching sales that only happen because her subscribers trust her. "Every newsletter I send out feels like a mini money-making machine," Lin says. "But beyond the ads, what's even more rewarding is the community I've nurtured — readers who trust me, learn from me, and buy from me."

That trust doesn't happen on a social feed. It happens in the inbox.

### 2. Use social for discovery, not dependency

The smartest creators use social platforms as a top-of-funnel channel, then move people to email where the relationship deepens. EssentiallySports converted their massive social following into 1M+ newsletter subscribers. Jorgenson did the same with LNI Media.

Social media is the lobby. Email is the living room.

### 3. Collaborate to borrow trust

When a creator your audience already trusts recommends you, the implied endorsement does more than any ad spend. Newsletter swaps, co-hosted events, and cross-promotions let you borrow credibility from adjacent communities.

Morning Brew grew to millions of subscribers partly through referral rewards. Even at a smaller scale, a simple referral program generates compounding growth because your fans are doing the recruiting.

### 4. Reward the people who show up

Founding member programs, early access to new products, public shout-outs in your newsletter — these small gestures convert subscribers into advocates. Tyler Denk's Big Desk Energy newsletter offers exclusive perks for referrers, including access to pitch decks and merch discounts.

The key: make rewards feel earned, not transactional. When fans contribute to your growth and get recognized for it, they become partners.

## What Keeps Fans Around

Building a fanbase is hard. Keeping one is harder. The creators who sustain loyal communities share five traits:

**Consistency.** Same schedule, same quality, same voice. Fans invest in creators they can count on. TLDR has published every weekday for years — that reliability is the real engine behind 48% open rates and 7M+ subscribers.

**Evolution.** Loyalty doesn't mean stagnation. Matt Brown expanded Extra Points from a newsletter into a document library, a video game, and university licensing deals — all by listening to what his audience wanted.

**Authenticity.** Fans detect inauthenticity instantly. Stay true to your voice as you scale.

**Reciprocity.** Exclusive content, early access, simple recognition — give back to the people who show up.

**Partnership, not transaction.** When fans feel like they're part of something rather than consuming something, they stick. They contribute. They advocate. That's the foundation of a sustainable creator business.

## The Bottom Line

A fanbase isn't built by chasing subscriber counts. It's built by earning trust, one email at a time. Own your audience. Go deep instead of wide. Reward the people who believe in you early.

The creators who figure this out — the Matt Browns, the Chloe Lins, the EssentiallySports — aren't just building newsletters. They're building businesses that survive algorithm changes, platform shifts, and market downturns.

Start with email. Build trust. The fans will follow.
