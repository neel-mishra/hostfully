# Why Your Emails Are Going to Gmail's Promotions Tab

Gmail sorts 1.8 billion accounts into tabs. If you send a newsletter, yours almost certainly lands in Promotions. That's not a deliverability failure — it's Gmail doing exactly what it was designed to do.

The Promotions tab exists to separate bulk and commercial email from personal messages. Newsletters, by definition, are bulk. They go to many people on a schedule with a consistent template. Gmail sees this and routes accordingly.

Here's what actually matters: Promotions is not Spam. Emails in Promotions are fully delivered, searchable, and visible. Emails in Spam are buried and damage your sender reputation. Conflating the two is the most common mistake newsletter operators make.

## What Triggers Promotions Placement

Gmail's algorithm weighs several signals simultaneously:

- **Sending volume and cadence** — large lists on a recurring schedule are the strongest trigger
- **HTML templates** — consistent formatting across sends signals commercial intent
- **Promotional language** — discount codes, CTAs, and marketing copy push emails toward Promotions
- **Low reply rates** — if recipients never reply, Gmail infers the relationship is one-directional
- **Engagement decay** — declining open and click rates over time can reinforce the classification

No single factor determines placement. Gmail evaluates patterns across millions of messages. A newsletter that sends daily to 100,000 subscribers with a templated layout will land in Promotions for most recipients. That's the system working as intended.

## Why Newsletters Almost Always Land Here

Newsletters share every characteristic Gmail associates with promotional content: bulk distribution, scheduled delivery, standardized structure, and passive consumption patterns.

TLDR sends to over 7 million subscribers daily and maintains open rates above 40% — well above the industry average of 15-20%. The tab placement doesn't prevent engagement. Habits do. Readers who build the habit of checking Promotions (or moving specific senders to Primary) engage at rates comparable to Primary inbox placement.

The newsletters with the highest open rates aren't the ones gaming tab placement. They're the ones readers actively seek out because the content is worth finding.

## When Promotions Placement Actually Hurts

Tab placement becomes a problem under one condition: your subscribers don't know where to find you.

If readers expect your email in Primary and don't see it, they assume it didn't arrive. They don't check Promotions. Open rates drop. Gmail interprets declining engagement as confirmation that the content belongs in Promotions. The cycle reinforces itself.

The fix isn't technical. It's behavioral.

## How Readers Can Move You to Primary

Five actions train Gmail to reclassify a sender:

1. **Drag the email from Promotions to Primary** — Gmail will ask to do this for all future messages from that sender
2. **Reply to the email** — two-way conversation is the strongest signal of personal importance
3. **Add the sender to Contacts** — explicit trust signal
4. **Star or mark as important** — manual priority indicators
5. **Open consistently** — sustained engagement teaches the algorithm over time

These are user-side actions. As a sender, you can prompt them — "Drag this to Primary" in your welcome email — but you can't force them.

## What Actually Drives Open Rates

Senders who obsess over tab placement are solving the wrong problem. Open rates are driven by three things:

**Trust.** Subscribers open emails from senders they trust. That trust is built by showing up consistently with content worth reading. Not by subject line tricks.

**Habit.** Newsletters that arrive at the same time every day become part of a routine. Consistency in delivery schedule matters more than inbox tab.

**Value density.** Every email should earn its place. If a subscriber skips three issues and misses nothing, the fourth won't get opened regardless of where it lands.

The Promotions tab is Gmail's way of saying "this is a commercial email." It's correct. Accept the classification and focus on being the commercial email that readers actively seek out.
